## About the class

Begin with plant growth and pasture composition, then work through recovery, paddock readiness, forage supply, herd demand, and rotation design. The final lessons connect seasonal management, pasture recovery, and a full year of grazing and hay budgeting. [Agronomy Fundamentals](../agronomy-fundamentals/README.md) provides the related soil background.

Cedar Run Farm is a fictional teaching example. Its herd, acreage, forage values, and calculations are illustrative, rather than measurements or recommendations for Tripple B Farms. The supplied PDFs have not been edited.

## Lesson 1: How Pasture Plants Grow

Grass anatomy, photosynthesis, and seasonal growth. Connect leaves, roots, and soil conditions with the way pasture plants develop.

## Lesson 2: What Grows in a Pasture

Identify the roles of grasses, clovers, and other broadleaf plants, and understand how grazing and growing conditions change a forage mixture.

## Lesson 3: What Happens After a Bite

Explore how leaf removal, stored carbohydrates, and repeat grazing affect plant recovery, and why equal rest periods can produce different results.

## Lesson 4: How to Tell When a Paddock Is Ready

Assess plant development, ground firmness, and the residual left after grazing. Recognize wet-ground damage and judge readiness across a paddock.

## Lesson 5: How Much Forage Is Available?

Estimate forage on a dry-matter basis, distinguish standing mass from usable feed, and build a budget that accounts for residual and grazing losses.

## Lesson 6: How Much Forage Does the Herd Need?

Estimate herd dry-matter demand and combine it with available forage to calculate herd-days and allocation size using worked examples.

## Lesson 7: Designing Paddocks and a Grazing Rotation

Distinguish stocking rate from stock density, plan fences and water access, and calculate rotation intervals while allowing for changing recovery needs.

## Lesson 8: Grazing Through an Upper Peninsula Year

Follow spring thaw, summer slowdown, fall stockpiling, and winter feeding, with attention to soil conditions, forage access, and nutrient distribution.

## Lesson 9: Why Pastures Thin Out

Use plant patterns, field history, and soil tests to investigate thinning pasture, then match changes in grazing, traffic, or reseeding to the cause.

## Lesson 10: A Year in the Life of a Grazing Farm

Bring the class together in a fictional farm’s seasonal forage budget, including changing strip sizes, fall stockpile, and winter hay inventory.
